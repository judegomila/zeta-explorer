# Even--odd multiplicity and Krylov simplicity reduction

## 0. Claim boundary and exact outcome

This note isolates the weakest finite algebra currently known to guarantee
that the lowest inversion-even eigenvalue of the source Weil matrix is
simple.  It proves four finite facts.

1. At every common eigenvalue, the even and odd multiplicities differ by at
   most one.  In particular, a multiple even eigenvalue must collide with an
   odd eigenvalue.
2. Parity noncollision is equivalent to two boundary Krylov determinants
   being nonzero.  Either one of those Krylov determinants already proves
   that the whole even spectrum is simple.
3. The central even Krylov determinant is exactly `1/N!` times the odd
   Krylov determinant generated by the source commutator vector `beta`.
4. True-ground parity separation has exact coefficient and boundary-pullback
   formulations which are strictly weaker than positivity at a higher Ritz
   shift.

These reductions do **not** prove any of the required actual-source
nonvanishing or sign conditions on an unbounded schedule.  Real-analytic
dependence between prime-power thresholds gives a conditional deterministic
selection procedure only after a nonidentity theorem has been supplied.

nonzero `beta`, inversion, and the rank-two commutator do not supply that
theorem.

## 1. Source-Cauchy parity setup

For `-N<=n<=N`, let

\[
 MV_n=nV_n,\qquad \eta=\sum_{|n|\leq N}V_n,
 \qquad \beta=\sum_{|n|\leq N}b_nV_n.                \tag{1.1}
\]

The complete source matrix has

\[
 Q_{nn}=a_n,\qquad
 Q_{nm}={b_n-b_m\over n-m}\quad(n\ne m),
 \qquad a_{-n}=a_n,\quad b_{-n}=-b_n.                \tag{1.2}
\]

Consequently

\[
 [M,Q]=|\beta\rangle\langle\eta|
       -|\eta\rangle\langle\beta|,\qquad JQ=QJ.     \tag{1.3}
\]

Write `H_+` and `H_-` for the inversion-even and inversion-odd spaces and
`Q_+`, `Q_-` for the corresponding restrictions.  In the normalized parity
bases

\[
 e_0=V_0,\qquad
 e_n^+={V_n+V_{-n}\over\sqrt2},\qquad
 e_n^-={V_n-V_{-n}\over\sqrt2},                      \tag{1.4}
\]

put

\[
 D=P_-M|_{H_+},\qquad De_0=0,\qquad De_n^+=ne_n^-.
                                                               \tag{1.5}
\]

Here `delta(f)=<eta,f>`, `delta(e_0)=1`, `eta` is even, and `beta` is odd.
The parity block of (1.3) is

\[
 \boxed{DQ_+-Q_-D=|\beta\rangle\delta.}             \tag{1.6}
\]

The physical logarithmic derivative is `(2*pi/L)D`.  The common scalar has
been omitted from (1.6), so using the integer-mode operator causes no change
in any spectral assertion below.

## 2. Symmetric multiplicity theorem

For a real number `mu`, define

\[
 E_+(\mu)=\ker(Q_+-\mu I),\qquad
 E_-(\mu)=\ker(Q_--\mu I),                           \tag{2.1}
\]

and let `k_+(mu)` and `k_-(mu)` be their dimensions.

### Theorem 2.1 (multiplicities differ by at most one)

For every `mu`,

\[
 \boxed{k_+(\mu)\leq k_-(\mu)+1,\qquad
        k_-(\mu)\leq k_+(\mu)+1.}                  \tag{2.2}
\]

In particular, an eigenvalue belonging to only one parity sector is simple,
and

\[
 \boxed{k_+(\mu)\geq2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
        \mu\in\operatorname{spec}(Q_-).}            \tag{2.3}
\]

**Proof.**  The subspace `E_+(mu) intersect ker(delta)` has dimension at
least `k_+(mu)-1`.  If `x` lies in it, then parity gives
`<beta,x>=0`, and (1.3) gives

\[
 -(Q-\mu I)Mx=[M,Q]x
 =\beta\langle\eta,x\rangle
  -\eta\langle\beta,x\rangle=0.                    \tag{2.4}
\]

Thus `Mx` is an odd `mu`-eigenvector.  This map is injective on the displayed
subspace: `Mx=0` would make `x` a multiple of `e_0`, while
`delta(e_0)=1` and `delta(x)=0` force that multiple to vanish.  Hence
`k_-(mu)>=k_+(mu)-1`.

For the reverse inequality, `E_-(mu) intersect beta^perp` has dimension at
least `k_-(mu)-1`.  Every odd vector is orthogonal to `eta`; for `y` in this
intersection, the same calculation gives `(Q-mu I)My=0`.  The restriction
of `M` to `H_-` is injective, so `k_+(mu)>=k_-(mu)-1`.  This proves (2.2).
`square`

### Corollary 2.2 (bottom simplicity from sign-free noncollision)

Let

\[
 \mu_+=\lambda_{\min}(Q_+).                          \tag{2.5}
\]

Then

\[
 \boxed{\det(Q_--\mu_+I)\ne0
        \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
        \mu_+\text{ is simple}.}                    \tag{2.6}
\]

No assertion about whether the odd spectrum lies above or below `mu_+` is
needed in (2.6).  The stricter comparison
`lambda_min(Q_-)>mu_+` is sufficient, but is not necessary.

## 3. Boundary characteristic ratio and Krylov determinants

Put

\[
 A=Q_+,qquad B=Q_-,\qquad d=N+1,                    \tag{3.1}
\]

and define

\[
 p(z)=\det(A-zI),\qquad q(z)=\det(B-zI).             \tag{3.2}
\]

For a vector `u in H_+`, let

\[
 \mathcal K_A(u)=[u,Au,A^2u,\ldots,A^{d-1}u]        \tag{3.3}
\]

denote the square Krylov matrix in the fixed normalized even basis.

### 3.1 Boundary determinant identity

Let `H_0=ker(delta)` and `R_0=I-|e_0><eta|`.  Since
`D_0=D|_(H_0):H_0 -> H_-` is invertible, (1.6) gives

\[
 D_0(R_0A|_{H_0})=BD_0.                              \tag{3.4}
\]

Taking the Schur determinant in
`H_+=C e_0 direct-sum H_0` yields the polynomial identity

\[
 \boxed{
 q(z)=\langle\eta,\operatorname{adj}(A-zI)e_0\rangle.}
                                                               \tag{3.5}
\]

Equivalently, off the even spectrum,

\[
 {q(z)\over p(z)}
 =\langle\eta,(A-zI)^{-1}e_0\rangle.                \tag{3.6}
\]

Equation (3.5), rather than a numerically sampled eigenvalue list, is the
source boundary determinant used below.

### Theorem 3.1 (resultant equals two Krylov determinants)

Using the standard convention

\[
 \operatorname{Res}(p,q)
 =a_p^{\deg q}\prod_{p(\lambda)=0}q(\lambda),       \tag{3.7}
\]

where roots are counted with multiplicity and `a_p` is the leading
coefficient of `p`, one has

\[
 \boxed{
 \operatorname{Res}_z(p,q)
 =(-1)^{d(d-1)/2}
   \det\mathcal K_A(e_0)\det\mathcal K_A(\eta).}    \tag{3.8}
\]

Consequently,

\[
 \boxed{
 \operatorname{spec}(Q_+)\cap\operatorname{spec}(Q_-)=\varnothing
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \det\mathcal K_A(e_0)\ne0\text{ and }
 \det\mathcal K_A(\eta)\ne0.}                     \tag{3.9}
\]

Either nonzero determinant in (3.9), by itself, says that its generating
vector is cyclic for the Hermitian matrix `A`; it therefore proves that all
even eigenvalues are simple.  Requiring both is stronger because it also
excludes every parity collision.

**Proof.**  First suppose `A` has distinct eigenvalues `lambda_j` and real
orthonormal eigenvectors `v_j`.  Put

\[
 \alpha_j=\langle v_j,e_0\rangle,qquad
 \gamma_j=\langle v_j,\eta\rangle.                  \tag{3.10}
\]

At an even eigenvalue, (3.5) and the rank-one adjugate formula give

\[
 q(\lambda_j)=-p'(\lambda_j)\alpha_j\gamma_j.       \tag{3.11}
\]

In the eigenbasis, each Krylov determinant is a Vandermonde determinant
times the product of the corresponding overlaps.  Multiplying the two
Krylov determinants gives

\[
 \det\mathcal K_A(e_0)\det\mathcal K_A(\eta)
 =\left\{\prod_j\alpha_j\gamma_j\right\}
  \left\{\prod_{j<k}(\lambda_k-\lambda_j)^2\right\}. \tag{3.12}
\]

Substitution of (3.11) into (3.7) gives (3.8), including the displayed
orientation sign.  Both sides are polynomials in the entries of `A` and in
the boundary vectors, so continuity extends the identity through repeated
eigenvalues.  The equivalence (3.9) follows from the defining property of
the resultant.  `square`

### 3.2 The source `beta` cyclicity identity

Define the odd Krylov matrix

\[
 \mathcal K_B(\beta)
 =[\beta,B\beta,\ldots,B^{N-1}\beta].               \tag{3.13}
\]

### Theorem 3.2 (central Krylov determinant transport)

With the parity bases ordered as in (1.4),

\[
 \boxed{
 \det\mathcal K_A(e_0)
 ={1\over N!}\det\mathcal K_B(\beta).}             \tag{3.14}
\]

Thus

\[
 \boxed{\beta\text{ cyclic for }Q_-
        \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
        Q_+\text{ has simple spectrum}.}            \tag{3.15}
\]

In fact the implication is an equivalence between `beta`-cyclicity for
`Q_-` and `e_0`-cyclicity for `Q_+`.

**Proof.**  Put `x_k=A^ke_0`.  Equation (1.6) gives

\[
 Dx_1=\beta,\qquad
 Dx_k=B(Dx_{k-1})+\beta\,\delta(x_{k-1})\quad(k\geq2).
                                                               \tag{3.16}
\]

It follows inductively that the columns
`[Dx_1,...,Dx_N]` are obtained from
`[beta,B beta,...,B^(N-1) beta]` by a unit triangular column operation.
The first column of `K_A(e_0)` is `e_0`.  After deleting its row and column,
application of `D` to the remaining shell coordinates multiplies the
determinant by

\[
 \det\operatorname{diag}(1,2,\ldots,N)=N!.          \tag{3.17}
\]

Equations (3.16)--(3.17) prove (3.14).  `square`

The condition in (3.15) is sign-free and is much weaker than positivity of
an odd shifted block.  It remains an unproved condition for the actual
arithmetic sequence `beta` on an unbounded schedule.

## 4. Exact true-ground separation criteria

The sign-free gate (2.6) is enough for simplicity.  If one nevertheless
wants the stronger true-ground ordering, set

\[
 T_-=Q_--\mu_+I,qquad
 C_{\mu_+}(s)=\det(T_-+sI)=\sum_{j=0}^Nc_js^j.       \tag{4.1}
\]

### Theorem 4.1 (equivalent true-ground separation tests)

The following are equivalent:

1. `lambda_min(Q_-)>mu_+`;
2. `T_->0`;
3. `c_j>0` for every `0<=j<=N`;
4. for every nonzero `f in ker(delta)`,

   \[
    \boxed{\langle Df,D(A-\mu_+I)f\rangle>0.}       \tag{4.2}
   \]

**Proof.**  The first two statements are identical.  If the eigenvalues of
`T_-` are `tau_1,...,tau_N`, then

\[
 C_{\mu_+}(s)=\prod_{j=1}^N(s+\tau_j).              \tag{4.3}
\]

Positive `tau_j` give positive coefficients.  Conversely, if all
coefficients are positive, (4.3) has no zero for `s>=0`, and its constant
coefficient is nonzero.  A nonpositive `tau_j` would give the zero
`s=-tau_j>=0`, a contradiction.  This proves equivalence with statement 3.
Finally, (3.4) transports `B-mu_+I` to the oblique boundary compression of
`A-mu_+I`; taking its derivative-metric Rayleigh quotient gives (4.2).
`square`

The comparison in Theorem 4.1 is at the **actual even ground**.  It does not
include the extra positive scalar `U-mu_+` present when an upper Ritz value
`U>mu_+` is used.  Hence it is strictly weaker than the historical
exact-`U` odd gate.  It is still a signed actual-source theorem and is not
proved here.

## 5. Real-analytic cells and the generic-schedule limitation

Fix `N` and an open interval of `lambda` on which the set of prime powers
`1<n<=lambda^2` is constant.  Equivalently, in the variable
`L=2 log(lambda)`, work between consecutive values `log(k)` with
`Lambda(k)>0`.  The closed pole formula is real analytic in `L>0`; after the
removable origin is regularized, parameter-dependent integration gives the
same property for the archimedean block; and every fixed prime term is a
finite analytic sine expression.  Thus every entry of `Q(lambda,N)` is real
analytic on that cell.

In particular, each of

\[
 \operatorname{disc}p,qquad
 \operatorname{Res}(p,q),\qquad
 \det\mathcal K_A(e_0),\qquad
 \det\mathcal K_A(\eta)                              \tag{5.1}
\]

is real analytic there.  If one of these functions is proved not to vanish
identically, its zeros are discrete.  A deterministic certified selection
is then possible:

1. enumerate finite-decimal dyadic rationals in a prescribed compact
   subinterval of the cell;
2. reconstruct the source matrix and the chosen invariant with outward
   arbitrary-precision balls; and
3. accept the first rational whose ball excludes zero, dovetailing the
   rational enumeration and precision.

The procedure terminates under the nonidentity hypothesis because dyadic
rationals are dense and a nonzero computable value is eventually separated
from zero by its balls.  It has no a priori precision or runtime bound.

This observation is conditional, not an unbounded schedule theorem.  Such a
schedule would require a proof of nonidentity on at least one relevant cell
in each of infinitely many unbounded parameter ranges, with the chosen
`N(lambda)` satisfying the separate projection requirements.  A certified
point proves nonidentity only on its own analytic cell.  Prime inventories,
the interval, and `N` change, so finitely many pilot points cannot supply the
missing statement.

At a newly admitted prime power `k`, put `h=log(k)`.  Its endpoint kernel
satisfies, for every pair of raw modes,

\[
 \omega_{nm}(h;h)=0,
 \qquad \left.\partial_L\omega_{nm}(h;L)\right|_{L=h}
 ={2\over h}.                                       \tag{5.2}
\]

Hence the right derivative of the complete matrix receives the rank-one
jump

\[
 -{2\Lambda(k)\over\sqrt{k}\log k}
 |\eta\rangle\langle\eta|,                          \tag{5.3}
\]

with the source minus-prime sign.  This exact jump does not establish
nonidentity: it is invisible on `eta^perp`, precisely where a multiple even
ground supplies collision vectors.

Even a successful generic selection would prove only qualitative
simplicity.  It gives no lower bound for the same-parity gap

\[
 g_+=\lambda_2(Q_+)-\lambda_1(Q_+).                 \tag{5.4}
\]

A selected rational may lie arbitrarily close to a degeneracy.  Therefore
the selection does not prove a residual-to-gap alignment rate, the signed
projective response, or locally uniform convergence.  It does not remove
ground alignment as the remaining frontier.

## 6. Exact structural counterfamilies

The following matrices are logical source-Cauchy models.  They are not
assembled from the arithmetic pole, archimedean, and prime inventory.

### 6.1 Persistent analytic multiplicity with nonzero `beta`

Fix `N>=2`, real `mu`, and `c>0`, and set

\[
 b_n=cn,qquad a_n=\mu+c\quad(|n|\leq N).            \tag{6.1}
\]

For `n!=m`, (1.2) gives `Q_nm=c`, and hence

\[
 \boxed{Q=\mu I+c|\eta\rangle\langle\eta|.}        \tag{6.2}
\]

The vector `beta=c sum_n nV_n` is nonzero and (1.3) holds exactly.  Since
`eta` is even,

\[
 Q_+=\mu I+c|\eta\rangle\langle\eta|,
 \qquad Q_-=\mu I.                                  \tag{6.3}
\]

Thus the even bottom `mu` has multiplicity `N`, and the odd eigenvalue `mu`
also has multiplicity `N`.  The odd Krylov matrix generated by `beta` has
rank one.  Taking `c=c(lambda)` to be any positive real-analytic function
produces a persistent analytic degeneracy; taking `mu>=0` even makes every
matrix positive semidefinite.  Therefore Cauchy form, positivity,
inversion, nonzero `beta`, the commutator, and real analyticity do not prove
the required nonidentity.

### 6.2 All three bottom-order outcomes already at `N=1`

Let `b_1=t`, `b_0=0`, `b_-1=-t`, and use the ordered raw basis
`(V_-1,V_0,V_1)`.  Then

\[
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}a_1&t&t\\t&a_0&t\\t&t&a_1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \nu_- =a_1-t,                                      \tag{6.4}
\]

while the normalized even block is

\[
 Q_+=\begin{pmatrix}a_0&\sqrt2t\\
                     \sqrt2t&a_1+t\end{pmatrix}.    \tag{6.5}
\]

These exact choices realize every possible relative order.

- With `t=1`, `a_0=3`, `a_1=2`, the odd bottom is `1`, below the even
  bottom `3-sqrt(2)`.
- With `t>0` and `a_0=a_1=a`, the even eigenvalues are `a-t` and `a+2t`,
  while the odd eigenvalue is `a-t`.  The even bottom is simple despite an
  exact parity collision.
- With `0<t<1`, `a_0=1`, and `a_1=1+t/2`, evaluating the even
  characteristic polynomial at `nu_-=1-t/2` gives `-t^2<0`; hence the
  even bottom lies strictly below the odd bottom.

Thus parity noncollision is sufficient but not necessary for even
simplicity, and no orientation of the two bottoms follows from the abstract
source-Cauchy identities.

## 7. Honest remaining target

For qualitative finite simplicity, any one of the following actual-source
statements on the chosen schedule would suffice:

\[
 \det(Q_--\mu_+I)\ne0,
 \qquad \det\mathcal K_{Q_+}(e_0)\ne0,
 \qquad \det\mathcal K_{Q_-}(\beta)\ne0.            \tag{7.1}
\]

The latter two are equivalent by (3.14).  They require no odd positivity.
If true-ground parity order is desired, Theorem 4.1 gives its exact stronger
target.  None of (7.1), Theorem 4.1, or the analytic-cell nonidentity is
currently proved for the complete actual-source matrices on a common
unbounded schedule.  Moreover, qualitative simplicity alone does not
replace the quantitative projective alignment estimate still required by
the limit bridge.
